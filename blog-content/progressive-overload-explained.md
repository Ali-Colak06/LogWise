Strip away the supplements, the influencer routines and the fancy periodization spreadsheets, and almost every successful training program runs on a single engine: **progressive overload**. Do slightly more than last time, recover, repeat.

## What it actually means

Progressive overload is the gradual increase of stress placed on your body during training. Your muscles adapt to what you ask of them — so if you ask the same thing every week, they have no reason to grow.

"More" doesn't only mean more weight. You can progress by adding:

- **Weight** — 60 kg → 62.5 kg
- **Reps** — 8, 8, 7 → 8, 8, 8
- **Sets** — 3 working sets → 4
- **Proximity to failure** — same set, but with 1 rep in reserve instead of 3
- **Control** — same numbers, better range of motion and tempo

Any of these counts. The mistake is chasing all of them at once.

## Why logging comes first

Here's the uncomfortable truth: **you cannot apply progressive overload if you don't know what "last time" was.**

Overload is a comparison. No record, no comparison, no overload — just random workouts that feel hard. A training log turns "I think I'm getting stronger" into a number that either went up or didn't.

## A practical weekly approach

1. **Pick one progression lever per exercise.** For most lifts, adding reps until you hit the top of a range (say 8–12), then adding weight, works for years.
2. **Before each session, check your last log.** Your target is written there: beat one number by the smallest possible margin.
3. **Don't force it every session.** Some days you just match last week. That's fine — the trend matters, not the day.
4. **Review every 4–6 weeks.** If a lift has been flat for a month, change something deliberately: more volume, a deload, or a variation swap.

## Common mistakes

- **Jumping weight too fast.** +2.5 kg on a press is a big jump. Microload or add reps instead.
- **Confusing soreness with progress.** Soreness measures novelty, not growth. The log measures growth.
- **Program hopping.** Any reasonable program works if the numbers go up. None work if they don't.

Progressive overload is boring, slow and completely reliable — the opposite of most fitness content. Track your lifts, beat last week by a little, and let the months do the heavy lifting.
